Dave Matthews Band is, for me, one of *those* artists—formative, personal, and just weird enough that they were never quite cool, but always interesting. They broke through in the mid-90s, but their sound doesn’t map neatly onto any genre of the time. Acoustic guitar, electric bass, jazz drums, violin, saxophone—*this is not a normal rock band setup*, and they didn’t play like one either.

When I think of them, I think of a band with **extraordinary musicianship**, a huge live following, and a kind of goofball sincerity that somehow works. These songs breathe, jam, twist, and explode. And for me, they were a big part of how I got into music seriously—as a listener, and as a guitarist.

This post is longer than most will be in this series, but it's an exception for a reason. The Dave Matthews Band—and Dave Matthews as a songwriter—was important to my musical development. Here's a dive into some key tracks across their early career, from radio hits to live staples to stripped-down acoustic gems.

### Ants Marching – Dave Matthews Band (1995)
*[Spotify embed placeholder]*  

One of their earliest breakout hits, “Ants Marching” shows off the **band’s unusual lineup** immediately: big violin and sax punches, Carter Beauford’s snare-heavy drumwork, and Dave’s strummed acoustic guitar pushing everything forward. The band sounds like it’s *having fun*, and that sense of joy—alongside the unique instrumentation—makes it instantly recognizable.

### What Would You Say – Dave Matthews Band   (1995)
*[Spotify embed placeholder]*  

This track shows off **Dave’s guitar playing**, often overlooked in favor of his vocals. The opening lick is quirky, rhythmic, and full of personality. The breakdown section later in the song gets **funky and loose**, highlighting the band’s jam roots. It’s one of the most playful studio recordings they ever made.

### Crash Into Me – Dave Matthews Band (1996)
*[Spotify embed placeholder]*  

This track is beautiful, romantic, and a little unsettling. Lyrics aside, it’s easy to get swept up in the *sound*—tender guitar lines, soft percussion, and Dave leaning into vulnerability. Famously featured in *Lady Bird*, the song is the punchline of a joke about teenage taste—but also, secretly, a gorgeous piece of songwriting. This was my entry point into the band, and I still love it.

#### Raw Notes
- Dave Matthews left apartheid era South Africa to come to Virginia in the US and tend bar. Forms band from there.
- DMB highest grossing live act for 2000 to 2009. Made half billion dollars touring.
- Album remember two things in 1993, "ants marching"
- First real studio album Under the Table and Dreaming released in 1994
- 1996 album Crash
- Greta Gerwig for movie later Lady Bird wrote Dave Matthews to get approval to use "Crash Into Me" into key moments of the movie. Character moments.
- Despite her describing it as "the most romantic song of all time", it is actually kind of creepy, a man lusting after a girl through a window

### Too Much – Dave Matthews Band  
*[Spotify embed placeholder]*  

Loud, theatrical, and full of swagger, “Too Much” is about indulgence in every form—food, power, lust, you name it. The rhythm section is on fire here, with Beauford’s drumming and Lessard’s bass taking center stage. I’ve written about this one in my *Music Made Me* series, and it still holds up as a track that taught me rock could be complex *and* catchy.

### “Stay (Wasting Time)” – Dave Matthews Band (1998)
*[Spotify embed placeholder]*  

This is Dave Matthews Band doing a **throwback soul number**, complete with full backup singers. There’s a Motown energy to it that’s not typical of the band’s sound, but it works. That guitar lick in the intro, the horn stabs, the joyous chorus—it’s all just *fun*. Less common in live sets due to the need for backup vocals, but a great studio moment.

### “Don’t Drink the Water” – Dave Matthews Band (1998)
*[Spotify embed placeholder]*  

Dark and dramatic, this is one of the band’s **most overtly narrative tracks**. It tells a story of colonization and displacement from the perspective of a sinister, unreliable narrator. I didn’t catch the full meaning at the time—lyrics were never my strong suit—but you can feel the **menace** in the music even without the words. It’s a bold, strange, powerful song.

## 🎧 *Live Recordings & Improvisation*

### “#41” – Dave Matthews Band  
*From the album Listener Supported (Live)*  
*[Spotify embed placeholder]*  

This is DMB in their element—**live, unspooling, and totally locked in**. “#41” starts soft and builds gradually, giving each band member space to stretch. It’s not a song with a tidy shape, and that’s the point. This version, from *Listener Supported*, is often considered the definitive one.

### “#36 / Everyday” – Dave Matthews Band  
*Live at The Gorge, WA*  
*[Spotify embed placeholder]*  

I’ve seen the band at The Gorge—*not this exact show*, but during the same era—and it remains one of the best live shows I’ve attended. This medley captures the vibe perfectly. “#36” morphed into “Everyday” over time, and in this performance you can hear both—the call and response with the audience, the looseness, the joy. This is what made DMB legendary live.

### One Sweet World – Dave Matthews & Tim Reynolds (1999)
*[Spotify embed placeholder]*  

This version strips everything down to just two guitars and a vocal, and the result is intimate and revealing. You can hear **every nuance** of Dave’s rhythm work and Tim Reynolds’ intricate counterpoint. It was a huge influence on me as a guitarist—proof that you could do a lot with just two people and two acoustics.

### Dancing Nancies – Dave Matthews & Tim Reynolds (1999)
*[Spotify embed placeholder]*  

Originally a big band song, “Dancing Nancies” gets reimagined here for two guitars. What’s fascinating is how Tim fills the **space left by the full band**—his phrasing, fills, and soloing work almost like a string section, wrapping around Dave’s rhythmic playing. It’s a masterclass in **acoustic collaboration**.
