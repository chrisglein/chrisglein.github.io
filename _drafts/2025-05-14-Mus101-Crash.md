Dave Matthews Band has always been kind of an outlier. They don’t really sound like anything else from the '90s. They have elements of rock, jazz, funk, folk, and jam band energy, but the way it all comes together is weird and specific to them. And for me, they’re personal—one of those bands that hit at the right moment and stayed with me.

This post is longer than most in this series will be, but that’s because Dave Matthews Band was important to me, especially in how I grew as a listener and a guitarist. Below are a set of tracks that span their early albums and live performances, ending with something that had a huge impact on how I play guitar to this day.

### Ants Marching – Dave Matthews Band (1994)  
<iframe src="https://open.spotify.com/embed/track/3RxpDJPmP0fMxPXTWbDafK" width="300" height="380" frameborder="0" allowtransparency="true" allow="encrypted-media"></iframe>

This is one of their initial breakout hits. It was actually recorded on an earlier album and then re-recorded for *Under the Table and Dreaming*. It’s a great way to introduce the band and what makes them unique.

The makeup of the band is not like a standard rock lineup. You’ve got Dave Matthews on vocals and acoustic guitar, Carter Beauford on drums—who’s a legendary drummer, really creative and very capable—Leroi Moore on saxophone, Boyd Tinsley on violin, and Stefan Lessard on bass. So you’ve got guitar, drums, and bass, which is typical, but then sax and violin are core members of the band, and that’s not normal for a rock band. You can hear that immediately in *Ants Marching*. There are these big percussive hits that are emphasized by the horns and strings, and it gives the whole thing this full, round sound.

There’s a playfulness here, both in the music and the lyrics. It really sounds like the musicians are having fun. And they’re all incredibly capable players.

### What Would You Say – Dave Matthews Band (1994)
<iframe src="https://open.spotify.com/embed/track/1HkZrTqcZNsacqOhuewf1t" width="300" height="380" frameborder="0" allowtransparency="true" allow="encrypted-media"></iframe>

This is a song that really shows Dave Matthews’ approach to acoustic guitar. Most people probably know him for his vocals, but guitarists know—Dave is an incredibly capable guitarist. He plays in this creative, percussive way that gives his songs a rhythmic complexity that’s kind of hidden under the surface.

*What Would You Say* has this really interesting hook at the beginning. It’s got this little bend in it that’s iconic. And then later in the song there’s this funky breakdown that just emphasizes the party vibe of the sound. It feels like the band is just jamming and having fun, and you can hear that all the way through.

### Crash Into Me – Dave Matthews Band (1996)
<iframe src="https://open.spotify.com/embed/track/2B5c8t0AEiijbgNHQRUApL" width="300" height="380" frameborder="0" allowtransparency="true" allow="encrypted-media"></iframe>

This is a beautiful song, even if it’s got some slightly concerning adult subject matter. But the tone of it is just lovely. It’s gorgeous.

There’s this whole story about Greta Gerwig using this song in *Lady Bird*—I think that’s the movie—where she talks about how it’s the most beautiful song she’s ever heard. And there’s a scene in the movie where the character is supposed to hate the song, or at least pretend to, but secretly they love it. That’s kind of perfect. It was clearly the breakout hit from the *Crash* album, and probably their most pop-accessible track.

This was actually the first time I heard the band. I didn’t start with *Under the Table and Dreaming*—I went back to that one later. It was *Crash* that hooked me.

#### Raw Notes
- Dave Matthews left apartheid era South Africa to come to Virginia in the US and tend bar. Forms band from there.
- DMB highest grossing live act for 2000 to 2009. Made half billion dollars touring.
- Album remember two things in 1993, "ants marching"
- First real studio album Under the Table and Dreaming released in 1994
- 1996 album Crash
- Greta Gerwig for movie later Lady Bird wrote Dave Matthews to get approval to use "Crash Into Me" into key moments of the movie. Character moments.
- Despite her describing it as "the most romantic song of all time", it is actually kind of creepy, a man lusting after a girl through a window

### Too Much – Dave Matthews Band (1996)
<iframe src="https://open.spotify.com/embed/track/0qVgerdsnW0POKov2005d8" width="300" height="380" frameborder="0" allowtransparency="true" allow="encrypted-media"></iframe>

This is one I believe I’ve already written about in my blog, in the *Music Made Me* series. So this is a good chance to bring that story back in.

*Too Much* is from *Crash*, and it’s got this swagger to it. It’s chaotic and funky and theatrical in a way that really stood out to me. It’s about excess—eating too much, drinking too much, feeling too much—and the song leans into that idea hard. It’s loud and messy in the best way, and it made me realize a rock band could be rhythmic and complex and still totally approachable.

### Stay (Wasting Time) – Dave Matthews Band (1998)
<iframe src="https://open.spotify.com/embed/track/1aV6EZ4ZOmQgk0pE0WBg6Q" width="300" height="380" frameborder="0" allowtransparency="true" allow="encrypted-media"></iframe>

This one leans heavily on having backup singers with Dave, which gives it kind of a throwback, maybe even a Temptations-era feel at times. It’s super fun and poppy, and a little different from what the band was becoming known for, especially in their live shows.

They couldn't really tour with backup singers just for one song, so I feel like this one didn’t show up as much live, or had to be transformed quite a bit when they did it. But it’s a fun song. You can still hear Dave’s creative guitar style in the intro, and there are strong sax hits that really sell the throwback backup singer vibe.

### Don’t Drink the Water – Dave Matthews Band (1998)
<iframe src="https://open.spotify.com/embed/track/5mcuarMck5WWB83JmppRUR" width="300" height="380" frameborder="0" allowtransparency="true" allow="encrypted-media"></iframe>

Totally different sound from anything we’ve talked about so far. This is Dave doing storytelling, and what’s interesting is that it’s a story told from an untrustworthy narrator.

When I first heard this song, back in '97 or '98, I had no idea what it was about. I’m not great at understanding lyrics—I usually process melody first and lyrics last—but even then, I could feel the sinister vibe in this one. There’s something dark and conflicted in the music, and it really comes through.

It took me an embarrassingly long time to figure out the real meaning. It’s a song about colonization and native peoples being pushed out. That context completely changed how I hear the track now, and it made me appreciate how bold it was for them to put this out as a single.

### #41 (Live) – Dave Matthews Band (1999)
<iframe src="https://open.spotify.com/embed/track/4e7lovbZc0GTEzMvJs2psb" width="300" height="380" frameborder="0" allowtransparency="true" allow="encrypted-media"></iframe>

Dave Matthews Band became known for their live act too, and “#41” from *Listener Supported* is a great example of what makes their live performances special.

The song already had a lot of space in its studio version, but live, it stretches out and breathes. Each musician gets a moment. Beauford’s drumming is conversational. It’s looser and more expressive, but still incredibly tight. A lot of people consider this the definitive version of the song, and I think that’s fair.

### #36 / Everyday (Live at The Gorge) – Dave Matthews Band (2002)
<iframe src="https://open.spotify.com/embed/track/1c3MTLF9YvQoi9cPLQzMcY" width="300" height="380" frameborder="0" allowtransparency="true" allow="encrypted-media"></iframe>

This is from a recording at The Gorge in Washington, which is one of the most beautiful venues anywhere. I’ve seen Dave Matthews Band perform there—maybe not this exact show, but one from that same general time period.

What’s interesting here is how “#36” evolved over time into “Everyday,” and in this live performance you can hear both. The crowd joins in for the call and response parts, which is a big part of the energy at their shows. You really feel the band and the audience collaborating to shape the moment. It’s loose and joyful and intimate all at once.

### One Sweet World – Dave Matthews & Tim Reynolds (1999)
<iframe src="https://open.spotify.com/embed/track/6ktmDxQ2zAJqudGek6Vlqk" width="300" height="380" frameborder="0" allowtransparency="true" allow="encrypted-media"></iframe>

This collaboration was huge for me as a guitarist. It showed how songs could be pared down to just one or two acoustic guitars and still hold all their emotional weight.

*One Sweet World* opens the album, and you can hear how Dave and Tim build off each other—there’s real interplay happening, not just one person strumming while the other solos. It helped me think differently about how to play with other musicians.

### Dancing Nancies – Dave Matthews & Tim Reynolds (1999)
<iframe src="https://open.spotify.com/embed/track/7AeaPcmRr8foKnikUHDRZw" width="300" height="380" frameborder="0" allowtransparency="true" allow="encrypted-media"></iframe>

This one’s a great example of how a full band song can be reworked with just two acoustic guitars. Tim Reynolds does this amazing job of filling in the space that would’ve been taken by the rest of the band. He solos, supports, and lifts the whole thing up without ever stepping on Dave’s parts.
